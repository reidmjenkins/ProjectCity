### 2. The Route

Wren was at Choice by six, because Odile had told her to be, and because she had not really slept.

The doors slid open the moment she reached them. Inside, the shop was warm and bright and smelled of flour. The grain sacks stood in their perfect rows. A woman in a grey apron stood behind a counter at the end of a long white aisle, and above her head, on a small screen, Your Neighbor was eating toast.

"Band, please," said the woman, without looking up.

Wren held out her wrist. The woman passed a little wand over it. Something chimed.

"Four-four-seven-one-K. New arrival." Now the woman did look up, briefly, with the bored kindness of someone who has said the same sentence nine thousand times. "Your allocation for today is one loaf, standard; two potatoes; one apple; protein ration, one tin; tea, one sachet. You may choose any loaf from the shelf marked *Standard*."

The shelf marked *Standard* held forty identical loaves.

Wren chose the third one from the left. It felt important to choose something.

---

The Arrivals Courier Depot was a long, low building at the bottom of the block, with a wide roller door and a smaller door beside it that said **STAFF** and, under that, in smaller letters, **EVERYONE IS STAFF**. Inside, it was all noise. Conveyor belts rattled. Parcels in grey paper slid down chutes into wire baskets. Twenty or thirty couriers in grey jackets moved between the baskets and a wall of lockers, calling to each other, laughing, swearing mildly, and over all of it a voice like a ship's horn was shouting, "*Who* has taken my good pen? I am *asking nicely*."

The voice belonged to a big man behind a dispatch window. He had a round, red, cheerful face and a clipboard he held like a shield, and when he saw Wren standing in the doorway he stopped shouting and beamed at her as though she were a parcel he'd been waiting on for weeks.

"*You're* Four-four-seven-one-K!"

"Wren," said Wren.

"Wren! Marvellous. A bird. We've had a Robin, we've had a Finch, never had a Wren. Marcus Bell. Dispatch. Welcome, welcome." He reached through the window and shook her hand with both of his. His palms were warm and dry and enormous. "Did Teague tell you you'd be good at this?"

"He said I had a walker's legs."

"He says that to everyone. He's always right, mind. Strangest thing." Marcus checked his clipboard. "Right. Here's how it works, and it's very simple, which is why it's beautiful. You come in at seven. You take your bag." He reached under the counter and brought up a grey canvas satchel with a broad strap and a number stitched on the flap in white: **9**. "You take your manifest. You walk your route. Every parcel, you tap the recipient's band to yours, and it signs itself. No paper, no fuss. You're back by four. If you're on time, every parcel signed, nothing lost, nothing late, the system knows. And the system remembers." He tapped her band with the end of his pen. The **50** glowed. "Do that for a few years, clean, and you can apply for things. Anything. Better flat. Different assignment. A *farm permit*, even, if you like growing. That's the beauty of it. It's all right there in front of you."

"Nine," said Wren, looking at the bag.

"Route Nine. Arrivals Block, east side, from Tenement Ten up to the Checkpoint Three gate. Nine for Nine, ha! You're in Unit Nine, aren't you? I do like it when it lines up."

"Who had it before me?"

Marcus blinked. For a moment, just a moment, the big face went still, the way a pond goes still when the wind drops. Then he laughed.

"Nobody! It's a new route. Been unassigned. Block's growing, see. More neighbors every week." He looked past her and raised his voice to its full ship's-horn pitch. "*Kask!* Where is Kask? Somebody find me Jory Kask."

"Here," said a voice behind her. "I'm here. I've been here. I've been here for some time."

---

Jory Kask was eating a potato like an apple.

He was about her age, thin and quick, with dark hair that was still wet from somewhere and a grey jacket two sizes too big, the sleeves rolled up to show forearms covered in pale little scars like a cook's. He had a crooked, easy smile, and he aimed it at Wren as though they'd known each other years and she owed him money.

"Hello, new," he said.

"You're late," said Marcus.

"I'm *early* for tomorrow." Jory swallowed. "This her?"

"This is Wren. You'll walk her round Nine today, show her the stops, the short cuts, the lot. And Kask." Marcus leaned through the window. His voice dropped, became something almost fatherly. "*Only* the short cuts."

"I'm hurt," said Jory, and grinned, and took the bag from Wren's hands and slung it over her shoulder for her as though she were a coat stand. "Come on, Bird. Daylight's wasting and the parcels aren't getting any younger."

---

Arrivals Block in the morning was a different city from the one she'd walked through the day before. The pastel buildings shone. Screens on the corners showed Your Neighbor washing up, drying his hands on a tea towel, stepping out of shot, coming back. People moved in steady, untroubled streams toward the tram stops, the depot, the long white doors of Choice. The drones overhead were white again.

Jory talked the whole way. He talked the way some people hum, without seeming to need anyone to answer.

"Tenement Ten. Mrs. Abara, ground floor, likes you to knock twice or she won't come. Third floor, the brothers. Don't ask which is which, they don't know. Here—" He pulled her into a side passage so narrow they had to walk in single file. It smelled of damp stone and old soap. "Laundry Row. Cuts four minutes off the loop. Marcus loves a short cut, so you're allowed this one."

They came out the other end beside a long awning where steam rolled out of the vents of the block laundry and hung in the air like breath on a cold morning. Jory stopped under the awning and pretended to fix his bootlace.

"Look up," he said, very quietly. "Don't *look* look. Just look."

Wren looked up the way you look at weather. On the corner of the building opposite, high up, a small glass dome caught the light. On the lamppost at the end of the row, another. Above them, the drones drifted, slow and patient.

"Count them," said Jory.

"Two. Three, with the one on the gutter."

"Good. Now step back two steps."

She did. Under the awning, with the steam drifting across, the dome on the corner was blocked by the lip of the canvas, and the one on the lamppost was hidden by the laundry's own sign. The drones were just a white blur through the steam.

"Huh," said Wren.

"Pallet Lane," said Jory. He stood up, finished with his perfectly tied bootlace. "Not that you need to know. Just, you know. For your own interest. A woman likes to know where she's standing." He started walking again before she could say anything. "Tenement Eleven. Don't use the lift, it reports you if you lean on the walls."

---

The parcels were all wrapped in the same grey paper and sealed with the same blue tape, and none of them had anything written on the outside except a band number.

"What's in them?" Wren asked, around noon, when they stopped to sit on a bench with their issued lunches.

"Gratitude," said Jory, with his mouth full.

"Really."

"Really. Replacement kettles. Socks. Shoe insoles in the correct size. Wake for the folks who can't get down to the kiosk. Birthday allotments." He waved his bread at the street. "Freehold provides. We're the bit of Freehold that walks up the stairs."

That afternoon she watched a man in Tenement Twelve open his parcel on the doorstep, a new kettle, standard, exactly like his old one, and press it to his chest, and look up at the screen at the end of his corridor, where Your Neighbor was reading a newspaper, and say, "*Thank* you," with tears in his eyes. Your Neighbor turned a page.

Wren tapped his band. Her band chimed. *Delivered*. She found she couldn't think of anything to say, so she said, "Enjoy your kettle," and he said, "Oh, I will," and she believed him completely.

---

The last stop on Route Nine was a guardhouse at the Checkpoint Three gate, where Arrivals Block ended and the old stone streets of the Old Market began.

"Just a drop," said Jory. "They get the post like everyone else. Bureau of Liberty runs on forms." He handed her the parcel. "You do this one. Be nice. He's all right."

The young officer from the day before was leaning on the rail with his cap pushed back. Up close he was older than she'd thought, thirty or so, with tired eyes and a shaving cut on his jaw. His name badge said **MIRREN**.

"Courier," he said. "New?"

"Yes. Wren. I've got a parcel for the—"

"Bag, please."

She hesitated. Jory, behind her, said nothing at all, which from Jory was very loud.

"It's routine," said Mirren, not unkindly. He held out his hand. "Everyone going in or out of the gate. Even couriers. Especially couriers, if I'm honest, but don't tell Marcus I said that."

She gave him the bag. He opened it and looked inside without much interest: the manifest, her empty lunch paper, the guardhouse parcel. He lifted the flap and ran his thumb along the seam of the bottom, a bored, practised motion, and handed it back.

"Wake?"

"Sorry?"

"Your allotment." He held out his hand again. "Just need to see it."

She found the little white box in her coat pocket and showed him. He nodded and waved it away, and his band and hers exchanged a soft chime. Wren looked down at her wrist. The number hadn't changed. Under it, for a second, a small word glowed and faded: *Clear.*

"There," said Mirren. "That's a clean stop. Now you know what one feels like." He took the guardhouse parcel and tapped her band with his to sign for it. "Mostly nothing. That's how you want it."

As he turned, Wren saw the inside of his cap. Tucked into the band was a child's drawing on scrap paper: a stick man in a cap, holding the hand of a smaller stick figure, under a big, wobbly sun. In the corner, a name in purple wax: **PIA**.

"Is that your daughter?" Wren asked, before she could stop herself.

Mirren's hand went to the cap, quick, as though she'd seen something she shouldn't. Then his shoulders came down.

"Yeah," he said. "She's six. Got a cough." He put the cap back on and pulled it low. "Kiosk says it's malaise. Gives her the little ones. The children's Wake." He shrugged. "She sleeps a lot."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. It's provided." He looked past her at Jory, and something in his tired face got sharper for a second, then let go. "Afternoon, Kask."

"Officer," said Jory, sweetly.

"Your bag's very light today."

"I'm training. Showing her the ropes. Teaching her the honest life."

"Mm," said Mirren. "Go on, then. Both of you. You've got two hours of daylight and four of curfew."

---

"He likes you," said Jory, on the way back.

"He searched my bag."

"He searches everyone's bag. He *showed* you how he searches it. Different thing." Jory kicked a pebble ahead of them down the clean pavement and then, as if embarrassed, went and picked it up and put it in his pocket so it wouldn't lie there making the street untidy. "Clean stops are good, Bird. Remember how that felt. Boring. Boring is the best feeling in Freehold."

She thought about the inside of the cap. The stick man and the stick girl and the enormous sun, which was something you mostly couldn't see here, with the drones and the haze and the high close buildings. She wondered when Pia had last seen it. She wondered if Pia was awake enough to remember.

"Jory," she said. "Who had Route Nine before me?"

He didn't answer right away. He walked a few more steps with his hands in his pockets, rolling the pebble between his fingers. Up ahead, on the corner screen, Your Neighbor was watering a plant on a windowsill. It was the only plant Wren had seen since she arrived.

"Marcus tell you it was new?" said Jory.

"Yes."

"Well, then." He smiled at her, his easy crooked smile, but it didn't quite reach the rest of his face. "It's new."

---

At four o'clock Wren handed her bag back across the dispatch window, every parcel signed, nothing lost, nothing late. Marcus checked his clipboard and lit up like a lamp.

"Perfect shift! *Perfect.* First day! Do you know how rare that is? Kask, do you know how rare that is?"

"I've heard," said Jory.

"Look at your band, Wren."

She looked. The **50** flickered and became **51**.

"There it is," said Marcus softly, almost reverently, as if he'd watched a seed come up. "That's you, that is. That's all you. Nobody gave you that. You earned it." He beamed. "Keep it clean, love. Keep it clean and Freehold will open up for you like a flower."

She walked home through the warm evening with her loaf from that morning under her arm. Her legs ached in a good way. The number on her wrist glowed. Fifty-one. It was a stupid thing to feel proud of, and she felt proud of it anyway, and she let herself.

On the fourth floor of Tenement Fourteen, the door of Unit Eight was open a crack. As Wren passed, a hand appeared in the gap holding a folded napkin. Inside the napkin was a biscuit, still warm.

"For the walker," said Odile's voice. The door closed.

Wren ate the biscuit standing at her window, looking across the gap at the blue glow of other people's screens. At eight o'clock Your Neighbor told her that she had done well today, that every single one of them had, and she found that it was nice to hear, even from him.

At nine the bell rang and the drones turned red.

Before she lay down, she knelt and looked under the bed, the way you look under a bed in a new place, a little ashamed of yourself. There was nothing there but dust. The wall behind the bed was plain painted plaster, sage green like the outside of the building, cool under her hand. One panel near the floor sat a hair's breadth proud of the others, as if it had been taken off once and put back by someone in a hurry.

She pressed it. It didn't move.

*New route*, she thought. *New flat. New city. Nobody before me.*

She lay down and pulled the thin issued blanket up to her chin and told herself she was being silly, and after a long time she believed it enough to sleep.
