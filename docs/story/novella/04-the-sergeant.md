### 4. The Sergeant

By the end of her first month, Wren had a rhythm, and the rhythm had a secret in it.

Most days were clean. Most days she took her own bag, the new one with the stiff white **9** on the flap, and walked her route, and tapped bands, and came back by four, and watched her number tick up: fifty-two, fifty-three, fifty-four. Marcus called her "my little lark" now and pinned her name on the cork board under a paper star. Mrs. Abara in Tenement Ten had started leaving a boiled sweet on her step. The brothers on the third floor had worked out which of them was which, and told her, and then argued about it.

But on rainy days, or misty ones, or the hot heavy days when the smog came down off the Works and the drones climbed above it, she went to her locker and took out the other bag, the soft faded one, and something went into the bottom of it. Not every week. Not always for Pell. Sometimes a packet for the man in Tenement Six. Sometimes a slim flat bundle that felt like paper, for a woman in Eleven who never said a word, just held out her wrist. Once, something small and heavy and cold that Wren didn't ask about and Pell didn't explain, which made Pell draw a three on the slate, and which Wren carried with her heart going like a bird's.

She learned the weather like a sailor. She learned which corners had domes and which had only the brackets where domes used to be. She learned that the drones flew lower on rally days and higher on cold ones. She learned that Mirren had two days a week off, Tuesdays and Fridays, and that she did nothing on those days, nothing at all.

The tea caddy on Odile's shelf grew heavy again. On wet evenings the two of them sat in the room full of kept things and drank from the bird-beaked pot, and Odile told her stories about nothing in particular: a dog she'd had, a dance she'd been to, a boy with a bicycle. Each story stopped, always, just before it reached anywhere Wren might have recognized.

Wren's Standing was fifty-six. She had thirty-one scrip in a sock in the wardrobe. And she had not taken a single Wake.

It felt, most days, like the start of a life.

---

It was a Wednesday when it went wrong, which was the worst part, because Wednesdays were Mirren's.

It wasn't even raining. It was one of those flat, white, hazy days when the sky looked like the inside of an eggshell and the drones were blurs and the whole city seemed to have the volume turned down. Wren had the soft bag. In the bottom of it was a two: a hundred grams of Pell's coffee, for the woman in Eleven, who was going to pass it on to someone Wren would never meet.

Jory had his bag, number Six, and Wren knew from the way he carried it, a little too easily, that there was something in his too. He didn't tell her what. She didn't ask. That was another thing she'd learned.

They walked the last stretch together, the way they often did, Jory talking and Wren half listening, toward the guardhouse at Checkpoint Three.

Mirren wasn't there.

Three officers stood at the gate instead. Two of them were young and heavy and bored, with the bright new badges of men who had joined last season. The third was a woman.

She was not tall. She was not loud. She was standing with her hands clasped loosely behind her back, in a dark Bureau of Liberty coat that fitted her as though it had been made around her, buttoned to the throat. Her gloves were grey. Her hair was cut short and neat and was iron-colored at the temples. She wasn't looking at the queue of people waiting at the gate. She was looking at their *feet*.

Jory said something very quietly that Marcus would not have liked.

"Is that—"

"Grane," said Jory. "Keep walking. Don't slow down. Slowing down is the worst thing you can do."

Wren kept walking. Her legs had turned into someone else's legs.

---

"Couriers," said Sergeant Grane, when they reached the gate. Her voice was light and pleasant, the voice of a teacher who was never surprised by a wrong answer. "Six and Nine. Good afternoon."

"Afternoon, Sergeant," said Jory, with his best smile.

Grane did not return it. She looked at Jory's boots, which were scuffed on the outside of the heels. She looked at Wren's, which were wet at the toes, although it hadn't rained in two days.

"Where's Officer Mirren today?" Jory asked.

"Reassigned for the week." Grane's eyes came up, slowly, and settled on Wren's face. They were a pale, clear brown, like weak tea. "You're new, Nine."

"A month," said Wren.

"A month and four days." Grane didn't look at a screen or a clipboard. She just knew. "Your manifest takes you north on Market Approach, east at Tenement Twelve, and back along the main road. That's the efficient route. It's the one Dispatch gave you." She paused. "But on three days out of the last ten, your band says you went through Pallet Lane instead. Laundry Row. Behind the vents. It's four minutes shorter, I'm told." Another pause, polite, very slight. "It's also the only stretch of your route where the cameras don't overlap."

The world went very quiet.

"Is it?" said Wren.

"It is," said Grane. "And all three of those days were overcast." She held out her grey-gloved hand. "Bags, please. Both of you."

---

The young officers took Jory first. Wren had to watch.

They did it slowly and thoroughly, laying everything out on the fold-down shelf of the guardhouse: his manifest, his issued lunch paper, a stub of pencil, the pebble he'd picked up off the pavement on her first day, which he apparently still carried. Grane didn't touch any of it. She stood a little apart and watched Jory's face while they did it, not the bag.

Jory's face was cheerful and bored. It was a very good face. Wren had never been so frightened for anyone.

Then he did something stupid.

As one of the young officers lifted the bag to turn it over, Jory leaned on the shelf, casual, friendly, and slid his manifest an inch toward Grane, and Wren saw what was folded underneath it: a fan of scrip, five or six notes, peeking out like the edge of a card.

"Look," said Jory, lightly, "it's been a long day, Sergeant, and I know you lot are busy—"

Grane looked down at the scrip. She didn't pick it up. She reached out with one gloved finger and pushed it back across the shelf until it was sitting in front of Jory again.

"Attempted inducement of a Bureau officer," she said, in the same pleasant voice, "is a separate line on the form. I'd rather not have to write it. Would you?"

Jory's smile didn't move. Only his eyes did. Wren saw them flick once to her and then away.

"No, Sergeant."

"Then put that away." She nodded to the young officer. "Press the corners."

He pressed the corners. The second floor of Jory's bag gave under his thumb like a sigh. He tore the lining back and there it was: two flat paper packets, and the smell came up out of the bag into the warm still air of the checkpoint, dark and sweet and unmistakable, and the people in the queue behind them all went very still and looked anywhere else.

"Coffee," said Grane. She didn't sound angry. She sounded, if anything, a little disappointed, the way Marcus would have sounded. "Two hundred grams. That's not personal use, Courier Six. That's commerce."

"It's for my grandmother," said Jory.

"Your grandmother," said Grane, "was relocated to Outer Settlement Four eleven years ago, and is very well, and is lactose intolerant, and does not drink coffee." She held out her hand for his wrist.

---

Wren didn't decide to speak. She just heard herself doing it.

"He's lying, Sergeant."

Jory turned his head and stared at her. Grane's eyes moved, slow as a clock hand, from his wrist to Wren's face.

"It's not his grandmother," said Wren. Her voice sounded very far away, and very calm, and she had no idea where either quality had come from. "It's my neighbor's. She's seventy-six. Unit Eight, Tenement Fourteen. She doesn't sleep well and she won't take her Wake, she says it gives her dreams. She used to drink coffee before—" *Before the Settlement.* She didn't say it. "—when she was young. Jory knew I'd been worried about her. He was trying to do something nice. He's an idiot," she added, which was true, "but he wasn't selling it. He was going to give it to me. For her."

It was nearly true. It was so nearly true that she felt it might be.

Grane looked at her for a long time. Wren made herself look back. She thought about the long white desk and Teague's still face and his finger on the glass, and she understood suddenly that Grane was doing the same thing, but without a glass. Weighing her. Turning her over. Pressing the corners.

"You walk through Pallet Lane," said Grane. "Why?"

And Wren, who had prepared a dozen lies for this moment, every night for a month, lying in the dark in Unit Nine, found that she didn't want to tell any of them.

"Because you can see the sky," she said.

Something flickered in the sergeant's face. It was gone so quickly that Wren couldn't have said what it had been.

"At the end of Laundry Row," said Wren, "where it comes out onto the lane, the buildings stop for a bit. There's that gap where the old block came down. You can see a bit of sky. On a grey day it's just grey, but it's a lot of it. I grew up on the grain lines. You could see all of it there." She swallowed. "I miss it. That's all."

The checkpoint was so quiet that Wren could hear the hum of the drone above them, idling.

Grane looked at the coffee on the shelf. She looked at Jory. She looked, for a moment, at Wren's soft, faded, slightly-too-heavy bag, still on Wren's shoulder. Her gaze rested on the corners.

Then she held out her hand.

"Wake, Nine."

Wren's hand went to her coat pocket. It went in, and found lint and the stub of a pencil and nothing else, and the bottom of her stomach dropped through the floor of the city into whatever lay beneath it. The little white box was on her windowsill, beside her mother's thimble. She'd taken it out that morning to look at the green leaf on the lid, and thought about Odile, and forgotten it.

"I don't—" she said. "I left it at home."

"Mm," said Grane.

She took Wren's wrist in her grey-gloved fingers, quite gently, and touched her own band to it. Wren's band chimed. The number on her wrist flickered: fifty-six became fifty-five.

"Carry your allotment, Courier," said Grane. "It's expected." She let go. She turned to Jory and took his wrist the same way, and his band chimed three times, a falling sound, and Wren saw the number on his wrist drop like a stone, ten, twelve points. "Two hundred grams, confiscated. A fine of sixty scrip, to be withdrawn from your allowance. One mark, possession. And I'm noting the inducement as *declined, not offered*," she added, "since your friend was so honest with me."

"Thank you, Sergeant," said Jory, in a voice Wren had never heard from him, small and hoarse.

Grane picked up the two paper packets and handed them to one of the young officers without looking at him. Then she turned back to Wren. Her eyes went once more, slowly, to the faded bag.

"I'll remember you, Nine," she said. "Both of you." She stepped back from the gate and clasped her hands behind her again. "Enjoy your sky."

---

They didn't speak until they were back through Laundry Row and under the awning on Pallet Lane, where the steam rolled out of the vents and the cameras didn't overlap and nobody could see them. Then Jory stopped walking, and put his back against the wet wall, and slid down it until he was sitting on the pavement with his knees up.

"*Sixty scrip,*" he said, and laughed, a terrible cracked little laugh. "That's three months."

"I'm sorry."

"*You're* sorry." He looked up at her. "Bird. You *talked* to her. Nobody talks to Grane. You just stood there and—" He shook his head. "And she didn't even look in yours. Did she? She didn't open it. With *that* in it."

"She looked," said Wren. She sat down beside him on the wet ground. Her legs had decided, belatedly, to stop working. "She looked at the corners. She knew."

"Then why—"

"I don't know." She tipped her head back against the wall. At the end of the lane, past the gap where the old block had come down, there it was: a wide flat sheet of white-grey nothing, the eggshell sky, with drones crawling across it like flies on a window. It wasn't beautiful. It was just *there*, and big, and nobody had issued it. "I don't know why."

They sat for a while.

"Your neighbor really won't take her Wake?" said Jory eventually.

"Shut up, Jory."

"Right. Yes. Shutting up."

---

Mirren came back to the gate the following Monday, looking more tired than ever, with a fresh shaving cut and a new drawing in his cap.

Wren took her own clean bag that day. When he ran his thumb along the seam and asked for her Wake, she held up the little white box before he'd finished the word, and he nodded and almost smiled.

"Heard you met the sergeant," he said, low.

"She was very polite."

"She's always very polite." He handed back her bag. "That's what you want to be scared of."

Wren hesitated. Then, carefully, as if picking her way across a floor that might give, she said, "How's Pia's cough?"

Mirren's face shut and opened again.

"Same," he said. "Kiosk upped the dose. She slept eighteen hours on Saturday." He tried to make it sound like nothing. "She's fine. It's provided."

Wren nodded. She went home. She didn't stop thinking about it all evening.

The next rainy day, she went to the Old Market after shift and stood in the dim kettle-crowded shop, and asked Pell, very quietly, whether there was such a thing as medicine. Not Wake. Real medicine. For a cough. For a child.

Pell looked at her for a long time with their steady, patient, adding-up eyes. Then they went into the back room. They came out with a small brown glass jar of something thick and golden, and a twist of paper with dried leaves in it that smelled sharp and green and clean, like the inside of a pine tree.

"Honey," said Pell. "From somewhere very far away. And thyme. Steep it, sweeten it, give it warm before bed." They set the jar on the counter. "I'd call that a one. Nobody looks for honey. Nobody even remembers what it is." They paused. "No charge."

"Why?"

Pell's mouth twitched. "Because I'd like a friend on the Checkpoint Three gate," they said, "and so would you."

---

Wren gave it to Mirren on a Thursday, wrapped in her issued lunch paper, in the bottom of her clean bag, so that when he ran his thumb along the seam it was the first thing he touched.

He went still. He looked down into the bag at the little brown jar, and the twist of paper, and back up at her.

She didn't say anything. She didn't know what to say. She just held out her Wake before he could ask.

For a long moment, Officer Dace Mirren stood at the gate of Checkpoint Three with a courier bag in his hands and a choice in front of him. Wren watched him make it. She watched it move across his tired face like weather. Then he took the lunch paper out of the bag, folded it smaller, and put it inside his coat, against his chest.

He handed her the bag.

"Clear," he said. His voice wasn't quite steady. "Have a good afternoon, Courier."

"You too, Officer."

The following week, when Wren came through the gate with the soft faded bag on her shoulder, and something two fingers deep in the bottom of it, Mirren took it from her, and held it for a moment, and handed it straight back without opening it at all.

"Pia's sleeping better," he said, not looking at her. "Twelve hours. Woke up Sunday and asked for an egg." He cleared his throat. "Wake?"

She showed him the box.

"Clear."

---

That night, Wren sat on her bed in Unit Nine and looked at her band. Fifty-five. One point lower than a week ago, and she had never felt further ahead.

Across the corridor, through the wall, she could hear Odile humming the song with no name. It was raining again, softly. The drones were high. Somewhere in the Old Market, a person with steady hands was mending a kettle. Somewhere in Arrivals Block a six-year-old girl had woken up on a Sunday and asked for an egg.

*I've done something,* Wren thought. *I've crossed a line.*

She waited to feel sorry about it. She waited a long time, lying on the bed with her hands folded on her chest and the drones blinking red outside her window and the screen above her head a patient, sleeping blue.

She didn't.

At eight the next evening, Your Neighbor told the whole city that the Bureau of Liberty was pleased to report an *encouraging rise in community spirit* in Arrivals Block, and a *small, temporary uptick in unauthorized goods*, which the Bureau was *watching with interest*. He lifted his mug. He smiled his tired, kind, sunburned smile.

"Nothing to worry about," he said. "Nothing at all. But it's nice, isn't it, to know someone's paying attention?"

Wren turned off the screen. It came back on by itself and finished the sentence.

"Rest easy. Your Neighbor is watching out for you."
