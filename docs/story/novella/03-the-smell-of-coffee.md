### 3. The Smell of Coffee

It rained on Wren's ninth day in Freehold, and that was the day Odile opened the door.

The rain came in the afternoon, thin and warm and grey, and the city changed under it. The drones climbed higher, to where the cloud swallowed them, and their lights became soft smudges. The screens on the street corners showed Your Neighbor in a raincoat, laughing, holding an umbrella over a sparrow. People walked faster. Laundry vanished from the lines between balconies as if a hand had swept them clean.

Wren came up the stairs of Tenement Fourteen at half past four, soaked to the skin, her bag empty and her boots squelching, and found the door of Unit Eight standing open.

"In," said Odile, from somewhere inside. "Quickly. Shut it behind you. Take those boots off, you'll ruin my floor, it's the only one I've got."

---

Odile's flat was the same size as Wren's and felt three times as large, because it was full.

There were things everywhere. Not issued things. *Kept* things. A chair with a crocheted cover in seven colors. A stack of books with their spines turned to the wall. A cracked china dog on the windowsill, wearing a paper crown. Shelves made from crates. A cushion embroidered with a house Wren didn't recognize, a narrow house with a round window and a green door. And everywhere, on every surface, small, careful, deliberate disorder, the kind that only happens when someone has lived in one place for a very long time and refused to be tidy about it.

On the windowsill, beside the china dog, sat a teapot with no lid. Growing out of the teapot was a small plant with bright, soft, glossy leaves.

Wren stopped.

"Don't stare at it," said Odile. "It's shy." She was at the cooker with her back to the room. "Sit. The chair with the cover. That's the guest chair. I've never had a guest who didn't sit in it, so don't be the first."

Wren sat. The chair creaked. She couldn't stop looking at the plant. It was so *green*. Nothing in Freehold was that color, nothing except the little leaf printed on the lid of the Wake box, and that, she realized now, had been a lie, a flat painted thing. This was alive. She could see the veins in the leaves.

"Basil," said Odile, still not turning round. "You pinch the top off and it grows two more. Like rumors." She took a towel from a hook and wrapped it round something on the counter, and then began to turn a little handle, round and round, and under the towel there was a crunching, grinding noise, muffled and steady. "The rain's good. The rain's very good. The drones go high in the rain, and the vents don't carry. You learn the weather, living here. You learn it like a sailor."

The smell came then.

It came out from under the towel and filled the small room the way water fills a glass, all at once, dark and rich and bitter and sweet, the smell from Wren's first night, the smell from the corridor, the smell that wasn't cabbage. It went straight into her and found something there that had been asleep. She didn't know she'd closed her eyes until she opened them.

Odile had turned round. She was watching Wren's face with a look of such naked, private pleasure that Wren felt she should look away from it.

"There," said Odile softly. "*There* she is."

---

It was coffee. Real coffee, Odile said, from real beans, grown somewhere, by someone, from a plant that had a name and a history and had never been near a Bureau.

She made it in a little dented pot with a spout like a bird's beak, heated slowly on the cooker. It came out black and thick and so hot it burned Wren's lip, and the first mouthful was so bitter she nearly spat it out, and the second was so good she nearly cried.

"Small sips," said Odile. "It's not soup."

"What *is* this?"

"It's coffee, dear. I told you."

"No, I mean—" Wren didn't know what she meant. She held the cup in both hands and looked into it. It was dark and she could see the ceiling light in it, wobbling. "Why does it feel like this?"

Odile sat down slowly on the edge of her bed, the way old people sit, as though lowering something precious onto a shelf. She had her own cup. She didn't drink from it. She just held it under her nose and breathed.

"Because it's yours," she said. "Isn't it? Right now, in your hands. Nobody issued it. Nobody counted it. Nobody's going to tap your band and write down that you had it." She smiled. "It's a cup of something that's *yours*. You haven't had one of those in a long time. Maybe ever."

*Something that's mine.* Wren remembered saying it to Teague, at the long white desk, and his finger moving on the glass.

"Where does it come from?"

"Oh, a friend." Odile waved her free hand vaguely. "There used to be places. Little rooms on corners, with tables out on the pavement and chairs that didn't match. You'd sit there all afternoon and argue about nothing, and someone would play music badly, and the whole street smelled like this. Before the—" She stopped.

She looked at the window. The rain was running down it in long, crooked lines. On the screen above her bed, which was switched off, Wren could see the two of them reflected faintly, an old woman and a young one, sitting in a room full of kept things.

"Before the Settlement," said Odile, in a different voice, a flat and careful voice, the voice of someone reading aloud from a leaflet. "When things were very hard and there wasn't enough to go round. We're very lucky now."

"Odile—"

"Drink your coffee, dear. It's getting cold, and I haven't got much left."

She nodded at a tea caddy on the shelf. It was painted with faded flowers. Wren could see, by the way Odile had lifted it down, how light it had become.

"How much?"

"Enough for a few more rainy days." Odile shrugged, small and brave and unconvincing. "My friend moved away. Or the prices did. Same thing, at my age."

---

"You smell," said Jory, the next morning, with enormous satisfaction.

They were in the depot locker room. The conveyor belts were starting up behind the wall with their rattling heartbeat. Wren froze with her jacket half on.

"I don't—"

"You do. You smell *wonderful*." He leaned closer and sniffed theatrically at her collar, and she pushed him away, laughing despite herself, and then not laughing, because he wasn't. "Eight's been brewing," he said, quietly. "Hasn't she? In the rain. That old witch. I love her." He picked up his own bag, number **6**. "Don't wear that jacket past the checkpoint today. Mirren won't care. Grane would."

"Who's Grane?"

"Sergeant. You'll know her when you see her." He hesitated, then shut his locker. "Come with me after shift. To the Old Market. There's someone you should meet."

---

Old Market began on the far side of Checkpoint Three, and it was older than Wren had imagined anything in Freehold could be.

The pastel paint stopped at the gate as if someone had drawn a line and refused to cross it. Past it, the streets turned narrow and crooked and the buildings were made of stone, real stone, grey and gold and black with age, with carved doorways and iron balconies and windows of all different sizes. Some of the stones had words cut into them, worn almost smooth, in letters Wren didn't recognize. The streets climbed and dipped and bent around corners for no reason. There were stalls in the arcades, selling issued goods arranged with a little more love than Choice bothered with: paper flowers, painted spoons, bootlaces in four permitted colors. People lingered. People *talked*.

"Fewer cameras," said Jory, walking beside her with his hands in his pockets. "The streets are too twisty. They'd need a thousand. And the drones don't like the arcades." He pointed with his chin at the stone ceiling over their heads. "Old Market's a place you can breathe a bit."

"Why don't they knock it down?"

"Who'd want to?" He grinned. "Tourists from the Heights come down and buy paper flowers. Says *authentic* on the sign. Very popular."

At the bottom of a crooked lane that smelled of hot metal and machine oil, under a sign shaped like a teapot, there was a narrow shop with a window full of broken radios. A bell over the door jangled as they went in.

The shop was dim and close and crowded with kettles, hundreds of them, on shelves and hooks and stacked in towers on the floor, dented and gleaming and cracked and repaired. Behind a counter made from an old door, a person in a leather apron was holding a radio up to their ear, turning a dial with great delicacy.

"Pell," said Jory.

"Kask." Pell put the radio down. They were narrow and soft-spoken, with grey-streaked hair cropped close and very steady hands, and their eyes went to Wren and stayed there, pleasant and patient, adding something up. "And a friend. Courier?"

"Nine," said Jory.

"Nine." Something moved in Pell's face, and went still. "Hello, Nine."

"My name's Wren."

"Hello, Wren." Pell smiled. "Kettle need fixing?"

"No. I—" She looked at Jory. Jory looked at the ceiling. "My neighbor makes coffee," Wren said. "She's running out."

Pell didn't blink. They reached under the door-counter, and brought up a small paper packet folded tight, and set it on the wood between them. Even through the paper, even over the smell of oil and hot solder, Wren could smell what was in it.

"Twelve," said Pell.

"Twelve what?"

"Twelve scrip. For a hundred grams. That's two weeks of rainy days for an old woman who drinks it slowly." Pell's voice was gentle and very clear, like someone explaining a recipe. "A month ago it was eight. Two months ago, five. Prices go up when people notice. And people are starting to notice."

Twelve scrip was nearly all of what Wren had. She'd been issued a small allowance on arrival, for "personal sundries." She had spent almost none of it, because there was almost nothing to spend it on that wasn't already provided.

"I can't—"

"No," Pell agreed. "Not yet." They glanced at Jory, a quick look, a whole conversation. Then they bent under the counter again and brought up a second thing, and put it on the door-counter beside the coffee.

It was a courier bag. Grey canvas, broad strap, a number stitched on the flap. *Nine.* It was a little older than hers, the canvas faded and softened at the corners, and it looked exactly, perfectly like the one Marcus had handed her across the dispatch window. Wren reached out without meaning to and touched it.

"Feel the bottom," said Pell.

She lifted it. It was heavier than hers. Only slightly. She pressed the floor of the bag with her fingertips, and it was firm and flat and ordinary, and then, near the seam, it gave, a fraction, the way a floorboard gives over a hollow.

"There's a second floor," said Pell. "Two fingers deep. Sewn in, not glued, so it doesn't crackle. Lined so it doesn't smell. A beat cop who runs his thumb along the seam won't find it. A sergeant who knows to press the corners might." They said it as plainly as if they were telling her the bag was waterproof. "That's what I'd call a two. On a scale of five."

"A scale of five," Wren repeated.

"I like people to know what they're carrying," said Pell. "Before they carry it." They reached for a scrap of slate and a stub of chalk, and drew five short lines in a row, and touched the first. "One. You walk a packet of beans down a quiet street in the Market on a rainy day. Nobody looks twice. If they do, you lose the beans and pay a small fine, and you go home." The chalk moved. "Two. You carry it through a checkpoint in a good bag, past an officer who knows your face. A bigger fine if you're caught. A mark on your band." The chalk moved. "Three, four. More goods. Worse streets. Busier hours. Officers who've been doing it longer. Five—" They tapped the last line and didn't explain it. "You won't see five for a long time. Most people never do."

"And what's this?" Wren asked. "What you want me to do?"

"There's a man in Tenement Six," said Pell. "On your route. He'd like a parcel. Not a grey paper one. If you took this bag instead of yours, tomorrow, and put his parcel in the bottom, under your manifest, and tapped his band like any other drop—" They shrugged. "Mirren's on the gate tomorrow. It's meant to rain again. You'd go through Pallet Lane. I'd call that a two. Maybe a one-and-a-half."

"And if I'm caught?"

"You'd lose the parcel. You'd pay forty scrip that you don't have, so you'd owe it to the city, and they'd take it out of your allowance for a few months. Your Standing would drop four or five points. And Marcus Bell would be very disappointed in you." Pell smiled. "That's the worst of it, at a two. I won't lie to you about it."

"And if I'm not?"

Pell pushed the little paper packet of coffee across the door-counter until it touched her fingers.

"Then that's yours," they said. "And ten scrip besides. And your neighbor gets her rainy days."

Wren looked at the packet. She looked at the bag with its secret floor. She looked at Jory, who for once in his life wasn't saying anything, who was standing by a tower of kettles and turning one slowly in his hands and very carefully not looking at her.

She thought about Odile holding the cup under her nose, not drinking it, breathing it in. *There she is.*

"All right," said Wren.

---

The next day it rained, as Pell had said it would.

The bag felt heavier than it was. That was the strangest part. She knew, she *knew*, that the difference was a few hundred grams, less than her issued loaf, but all morning it pulled at her shoulder like a stone. Every drone she saw seemed lower than usual. Every screen she passed seemed to turn its head. Your Neighbor, on the corner of Tenement Ten, was drinking tea in the rain under his umbrella, and when she walked past he laughed at something off-screen and she almost dropped her manifest.

*Breathe*, she told herself. *Boring. Boring is the best feeling in Freehold.*

She took Laundry Row. She came out under the awning on Pallet Lane, where the steam rolled out of the vents and hid the corner dome and the lamppost dome and turned the drones to white smudges in the cloud, and she stopped there, for a breath, as if to shift her strap.

*Count them*, said Jory's voice in her head. One. Two. Three. All blind.

She walked on.

The man in Tenement Six was small and bald and wore slippers with the toes worn through. He opened his door the width of his face and looked at her bag.

"Is it grey out?" he said.

Pell had told her the answer. Her mouth was so dry she could barely make it.

"Getting greyer."

He held out his wrist. She tapped it. The band chimed. *Delivered.* She lifted out her manifest, and pressed the corner of the bag's floor the way Pell had shown her, and it opened like a mouth, and she put a soft, heavy, paper-wrapped parcel into the man's hands, and he closed his door.

That was all. That was the whole thing.

She walked the rest of Route Nine with her legs shaking. At Checkpoint Three, Mirren took her bag, and ran his thumb along the seam of the bottom, a bored, practised motion, and handed it back.

"Wake?"

She showed him the box.

"Clear," said Mirren. "Go on. You'll catch your death."

---

That evening she climbed the stairs of Tenement Fourteen with a folded paper packet in the inside pocket of her jacket, under her heart, where it seemed to give off a warmth of its own.

She knocked on the door of Unit Eight. Twice, the way Mrs. Abara in Tenement Ten liked it, though she didn't know why she did that.

Odile opened the door. She looked at Wren's face and her eyes went sharp and worried at once.

"What have you done?"

Wren took out the packet and put it in her hands.

Odile didn't open it. She didn't need to. She lifted it to her face the way she had lifted her cup, and breathed, and Wren watched her whole small body go still.

"Oh," said Odile. "Oh, you *foolish* girl."

And then, to Wren's horror, she began to cry. Not loudly. Just a few tears, running down the creases by her nose, which she didn't wipe away. She stood in her doorway with the coffee pressed to her chest like the man with the kettle, and cried, and laughed, and shook her head.

"You foolish, foolish girl," she said again. "Come in. Come in before somebody sees. I'll put the kettle on."

Behind them, down the corridor, the screen at the far end showed Your Neighbor sitting at his kitchen table in the warm light of evening, smiling at something only he could see.
