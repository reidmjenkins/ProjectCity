# The Last Tenant of Unit 9

## Part One — Arrival

### 1. Everyone Has a Place

The relocation train didn't have windows, so Wren Adair spent the last hour before Freehold watching the woman across from her eat an orange.

It was a real orange. Wren knew because the smell filled the whole carriage, bright and sharp, and nine strangers turned their heads at once like dogs hearing a door. The woman peeled it slowly in one long spiral. She didn't offer anyone a piece. When she finished she folded the peel into a square, tucked it into her coat, and closed her eyes. Wren decided she liked her.

The speaker in the ceiling had been playing the same message since dawn. *Welcome, new neighbors. Freehold is glad to have you. Please remain seated. Please keep your relocation number visible. Everyone has a place.* After the hundredth time it stopped being words and became a sort of weather, something that happened to you while you thought about other things.

Wren thought about the bag between her boots. It held two shirts, a pair of socks with the heels worn through, a comb, her mother's thimble, and a tin with a paper inside that said **4471-K**. That was all. Everything else from the settlement had stayed behind, because the letter had said *Freehold will provide.* The letter had said it three times, underlined, as if anticipating an argument.

The train slowed. The orange woman opened her eyes. Somewhere below them, brakes screamed, and the carriage filled with a pale, sudden light as the doors folded back.

---

Arrivals Hall was the cleanest place Wren had ever stood.

It was enormous and white, with a ceiling so high that the drones up there looked like moths. Long lines of people shuffled toward a row of desks under a banner that read, in letters taller than a man, **WELCOME HOME**. There were screens on every pillar. On every screen, a man in a soft brown cardigan smiled down at them from a kitchen table, holding a mug.

"That's him," whispered a boy in the line beside her, to no one. "That's Your Neighbor."

He didn't look like much. That was the strange part. Wren had expected someone tall, someone with a voice like a bell, but the man on the screens had thinning hair and a slight sunburn on his nose, as if he'd spent the weekend in a garden. He looked like he might ask how your mother was doing and actually want to know. The sound was turned low. She caught only pieces: *...so proud of each of you... a fresh start... no one in Freehold goes without...*

The line moved. Wren moved with it.

The desk, when she reached it, was staffed by a man who seemed to have been carved rather than born. He had a smooth, unlined face and very neat grey hair, and he sat so still that for a moment she thought he was another screen. Then he looked up and smiled, and the smile was perfect, and he said, "Four-four-seven-one-K. Welcome to Freehold. My name is Officer Teague, Bureau of Opportunity."

"Hello," said Wren.

"Please, sit." There was a chair. She sat. He did not look at any papers, because there were no papers on the desk, only a thin glass panel that glowed faintly when he touched it. "You've come from Outer Settlement Nine. Agricultural."

"Yes."

"You worked the grain lines."

"Since I was fourteen."

"And your mother?"

Wren's hands tightened on her bag. "She died. Last winter."

"I'm so sorry." He did seem sorry, in a smooth and general way, the way a wall is cool. "That will be why you were selected. Freehold doesn't like to leave anyone alone." He touched the glass. "I'd like to ask you a few questions. There are no wrong answers."

She had been warned about this on the train, in whispers. *The interview.* No one knew what it was for.

"What do you want, Wren?"

It was not the question she expected. She opened her mouth and nothing came out. Behind her, the line breathed and shifted. On the screen above Teague's head, Your Neighbor lifted his mug.

"I don't know," she said finally. "Something that's mine, I suppose."

Teague's smile did not change. His finger moved on the glass, a tiny motion, like brushing away a crumb.

"What do you miss most about Settlement Nine?"

"The sky," she said, without thinking. "You could see the whole thing."

Another tiny motion.

"Have you ever taken anything that wasn't issued to you?"

"No," Wren lied.

He looked at her for a long, pleasant moment. Then he nodded, as if she'd passed something, or failed it in exactly the expected way.

"Thank you. That's all." He reached below the desk and brought up a small tray. On it sat a key, a folded paper, a narrow grey band, and a little white box with a green leaf printed on the lid. "Your residence is Tenement Fourteen, Unit Nine, in Arrivals Block. Your assignment is Courier, Arrivals Courier Depot. You'll report tomorrow at seven." He slid the paper toward her. "Directions."

"Courier," Wren repeated.

"You'll be good at it. You have a walker's legs and an honest face." He picked up the band. "Your wrist, please."

She held out her left arm. The band was cold, then warm. It closed around her wrist with a soft click, and a line of pale light ran around it once and settled into a small glowing number: **50**.

"Your Standing," said Teague. "Fifty is where everyone begins. It's not a judgment, only a measure. Be a good neighbor and it rises. It opens doors." He tapped the white box. "And this is your Wake. One week's allotment. You'll collect more from the Wellness Kiosk on your block. Take one in the evening, after the Word, and you'll sleep like a child."

"Do I have to?"

The question came out before she could stop it. For the first time, something in Teague's face moved, a flicker at one corner of his mouth, like a fish turning under ice.

"No one *has* to do anything in Freehold," he said gently. "That's rather the point. But you'll want to keep it with you. People will ask." He pushed the tray toward her. "Everyone has a place, Wren. You've found yours. Next, please."

---

Arrivals Block was a twenty-minute walk from the hall, and Wren took forty, because she kept stopping to look up.

The buildings were tall and close together and painted in soft, careful colors, peach and sage and cream, like the inside of a nursery. Every window was the same size. Every door had the same brass number plate. Laundry hung on lines between the balconies, and all of it was grey, the standard grey of issued cloth, so that it looked as if the street had been strung with rain clouds.

There were people everywhere and none of them were in a hurry. A man swept a step that was already clean. Two women in matching aprons sat on a bench outside a shop called **THE CHOICE STORE** and watched her pass with mild interest. A child chased a ball into the road and a drone dropped out of the sky, hovered at the height of his head, and chimed politely until he went back to the curb. Nobody else seemed to notice. Wren noticed. She watched the drone rise until it was a speck again, joining a slow constellation of other specks drifting above the rooftops, and she understood why there was no garden anywhere, not a window box, not a single weed in the cracks of the pavement.

At the end of the street was a checkpoint: a low gate, a booth, a young officer leaning on the rail with his cap pushed back. He looked tired. He looked at her band, then at her face, then away. She kept walking.

Tenement Fourteen was sage green with a cracked step. Unit Nine was on the fourth floor, at the end of a corridor that smelled faintly of boiled cabbage and floor polish. The key turned stiffly.

The flat was one room. A bed against the wall. A table, a chair, a sink, a two-ring cooker. A wardrobe with one coat hanger in it. A window that looked straight across the gap into another window, where a curtain twitched and was still. On the wall above the bed, a small screen glowed a soft, patient blue, waiting to be needed.

Wren set her bag on the table. She took out the thimble and put it on the windowsill. That was all she had to unpack. She stood in the middle of the room for a while, feeling the size of it, which was exactly the size of one person, no more.

*Something that's mine,* she thought. *Well. Here it is.*

It was clean. It was dry. No one in Freehold goes without. She told herself to be grateful and was surprised to find that she mostly was.

Someone knocked.

---

The woman in the doorway was small and very old, with white hair pinned up in a style Wren had only seen in pictures, and a cardigan buttoned wrong. She held a plate covered with a cloth.

"You're the new one," she said. "Nine. I'm Eight. Odile Fenn. I've brought you bread, because the Choice Store won't give you your ration card until Thursday and nobody tells you that, the idiots." She pushed the plate into Wren's hands. "You look like a sparrow that fell out of something."

"I'm Wren," said Wren.

"Well, *there* you go." Odile's eyes, which were a pale, clear grey, went past her into the flat and took inventory in about one second: the bag, the thimble, the empty hanger. "Mm. They always give Nine to the walkers. Courier?"

"How did you know?"

"Because it's always courier. They like the young ones moving about. Keeps the blood going." She said it lightly, as though it were a joke, but she didn't smile. "Can I come in? My knees don't like standing in corridors. Corridors have ears, anyway."

Wren stepped aside. Odile came in and sat on the bed without being asked, and the room felt suddenly less like a box.

And there was a smell. It came in with her, clinging to her cardigan, faint and dark and bitter and warm, like burnt sugar and wet earth and something Wren didn't have a word for at all. It was a smell from a story. She breathed it in without meaning to and Odile watched her do it.

"What is that?" Wren asked.

"What's what, dear?"

"That smell."

Odile looked at her for a long moment, the same way Teague had, measuring. But it was a different kind of measuring. Teague had looked at her like a form to be filled in. Odile looked at her like a door she was deciding whether to open.

"Cabbage," Odile said. "The whole building smells of cabbage. You'll stop noticing." She patted the bed beside her. "Now. Sit down and eat that bread, and I'll tell you everything the idiots don't. Curfew's at nine. The Word's at eight. Don't be in the corridor after nine, not even to go to the toilet, there's one at the end and it's watched. The Choice Store opens at six and the good bread's gone by half past. Don't talk to the woman in Four, she reports her own cat. Mirren on the checkpoint is a nice boy with a sick little girl; be kind to him. And the Wellness Kiosk—"

She stopped. Her eyes had found the little white box on the table.

"Ah," she said. "They gave you your Wake."

"I'm supposed to take one tonight."

"Mm." Odile reached over and picked up the box and turned it in her old fingers. The green leaf on the lid caught the light. "Lovely packaging. They put a lot of thought into the packaging." She set it down again, exactly where it had been, square to the edge of the table. "You'll do as you like, of course. Everyone does as they like in Freehold."

It was so nearly what Teague had said that Wren laughed, and then stopped, because Odile wasn't laughing.

"Do you take it?" Wren asked.

"I'm seventy-six years old," said Odile. "I sleep fine."

That wasn't an answer, and they both knew it, and something passed between them in that small room that neither of them said out loud.

---

At eight o'clock the screen above the bed woke up by itself.

Wren had been sitting at the table finishing the last of Odile's bread, which was dense and a little sour and much better than anything from the grain lines. The screen chimed three soft notes, and then the kitchen table was there, and the brown cardigan, and the mug.

"Good evening, neighbors," said Your Neighbor.

His voice was the most comfortable thing she had ever heard. It was low and slow and a little rough, like a voice that had just woken up from a nap in a chair. It filled the room the way the orange had filled the train.

"Long day today. I know. I felt it too." He smiled and turned the mug in his hands. "We welcomed four hundred and twelve new neighbors to Freehold this morning. Four hundred and twelve. Some of you are watching me right now from a room you've never slept in before, and I want to say to you: you did it. The hard part's over. You're home."

Across the gap between buildings, through the other window, Wren could see the same blue glow, and the shape of someone sitting very still in front of it.

"I know it can feel strange at first. New streets. New faces. Maybe a little lonely." He leaned closer, and his eyes, which were brown and slightly tired, seemed to look straight at her. "But you're not alone. Not here. Never here. Everyone in this city has a place, and everyone in this city has a neighbor, and that's me. I'm right here. Every night." He lifted the mug, toasting her. "Get some rest. Tomorrow's a fresh start. And remember..."

The screen seemed to warm. The light in the room turned faintly gold.

"Rest easy. Your Neighbor is watching out for you."

The screen went dark. In the silence, Wren heard, all around her, through the walls and floors and ceilings of Tenement Fourteen, the small sounds of a hundred people opening a hundred little white boxes. A rattle. A rattle. A pause. A glass of water being filled.

She looked at her own box.

She thought about Teague's finger on the glass, brushing something away. She thought about the drone, hovering at the height of the little boy's head, chiming. She thought about Odile setting the box down square to the edge of the table, and the dark sweet smell that wasn't cabbage.

She put the box in her coat pocket, where it would be with her if anyone asked. Then she lay down on the bed in her clothes and looked at the ceiling.

At nine o'clock exactly, out in the city, a bell rang once. The streetlights dimmed by half. Through the window she watched the slow lights of the drones change from white to a soft, attentive red.

Wren did not sleep like a child. She lay awake for a long time listening to Freehold breathe. Somewhere in the building, faint as a memory, someone was humming a song she didn't know.

And on the other side of the wall, in Unit Eight, someone was very quietly making something that smelled like morning.
